Harnessing Ecological Infrastructure to Make the Urbanizing World a Better Place for All

Ecological infrastructure refers to the interconnected network of natural, semi-natural, and structural landscape features that sustain essential ecosystem services and promote ecological resilience alongside the built environment. We employ a variety of approaches to achieve a common goal: leveraging biodiversity (and anthropogenic structures that support biodiversity) to it's fullest potential.

Mussels as Biofilters

Native freshwater mussels function as dynamic, living suspension feeders within river corridors. By filtering water to feed on suspended particulates, algae, and organic matter, mussel assemblages directly reduce turbidity and improve light penetration across benthic habitats.

Mussels also drive critical nutrient cycling by sequestering carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus into shell and soft tissue while depositing bioavailable nutrients onto the riverbed for benthic food webs. Deploying targeted assemblages at appropriate densities, we can harness biological filtration as self-sustaining aquatic infrastructure.

Stone Walls as Ecological Infrastructure

Dry-stone walls represent durable landscape features that perform vital ecological functions. These porous structures influence local microclimates, moderate surface hydrology, and anchor slope stability against overland runoff and soil erosion.

The intricate interstitial spaces between stones provide continuous linear corridors and microrefugia for small mammals, reptiles, amphibians, and specialized bryophyte and lichen communities.
